## Background

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a highly prevalent mental disorder affecting roughly 185 million people globally. Although roughly 80% of MDD cases occur in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), an estimated 85% of affected individuals in LMICs lack access to treatment. Furthermore, existing treatment options suffer from limited effectiveness, have detrimental side effect profiles, and must be taken daily, leading to adherence issues. Closing the MDD treatment gap will require interventions that are effective, low-cost, and scalable.

Recently, a large body of research has emerged around the treatment of MDD with psilocybin, a serotonin 5-HT<sub>2A</sub> agonist and naturally occurring psychedelic compound. Administration in clinical settings in combination with a supportive therapy regimen is referred to as psilocybin-assisted therapy (PAT). Results from 7 recent clinical trials have demonstrated high efficacy, with results from just 1–2 doses often persisting for 6-12 months.

Despite these promising results and a demonstrated need for low-cost and effective mental health interventions in LMICs, studies of PAT have thus far been conducted exclusively in high-income countries. Jamaica, a middle-income country with a high prevalence of depression and a substantial unmet need for treatment, is an ideal location for research focused on adapting PAT to LMIC contexts, as it is one of the only countries worldwide in which psilocybin is fully legal. While private treatment and retreat centers using psilocybin actively operate in the country, there are currently no PAT programs focused on expanding access to marginalized and low-income populations within Jamaica.

## Objectives

We are currently working to develop and pilot test a scalable and cost-effective PAT delivery model for the treatment of MDD in Jamaica as a representative LMIC setting. Our delivery approach is designed to be low-cost, efficient, culturally grounded, and adapted to the Jamaican context. This model will include protocols for recruitment, screening, participant preparation, dosing session conduct, and integration therapy. Through a pilot single-arm clinical trial, we seek to determine the feasibility, safety, acceptability, and preliminary efficacy of PAT using fungal biomass. This will involve the enrollment of roughly 20 participants diagnosed with MDD in Jamaica to assess pre/post changes in key depression measures, as well as other measures related to adverse events, feasibility, and acceptability.

## Approach and Partnerships

We will run this study through a partnership between the Duke Global Health Institute (DGHI) and the Caribbean Centre for Research in Bioscience (CCRIB) of the University of the West Indies (UWI), in Kingston, Jamaica. The CCRIB of UWI has a deep understanding of the local context and operates a GMP-certified laboratory on the UWI campus where they grow *Psilocybe cubensis* fungal biomass, with capacity to test and calibrate biomass for psilocybin content.

## Impact and Future Directions

This will be the first study to develop and test a delivery model for psilocybin-assisted therapy designed specifically for an LMIC health system. A successful research program in Jamaica focused on developing and testing a scalable, cost-effective delivery model could serve as a pivotal stepping stone towards scaling the use of psilocybin-assisted therapy in LMICs around the world. In the future, we will pursue large-scale research funding for studies to (1) assess the effects of extended integration therapy, (2) compare the relative efficacy of group therapy vs. individual therapy models, and (3) understand barriers and facilitators to programmatic implementation and scale-up.
